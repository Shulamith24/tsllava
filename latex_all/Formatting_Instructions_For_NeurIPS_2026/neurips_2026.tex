\begin{abstract}
    Few-shot time series classification (TSC) is a fundamental yet challenging problem in many real-world domains where labeled sequences are costly to obtain. 
    While pretrained large language models (LLMs) have shown strong potential for data-scarce learning by reusing knowledge acquired from large-scale pretraining, their benefits remain difficult to realize for TSC: time series are continuous numerical observations rather than discrete language tokens, and accurate classification requires sensitivity to both temporal dynamics and waveform morphology. 
    We propose \textbf{TimeMorph}, an end-to-end framework that adapts pretrained LLMs for few-shot TSC by grounding time-series evidence in a form suitable for language-model reasoning. 
    TimeMorph first constructs a dual-view representation of each sequence, where a temporal branch captures chronological variations such as trends and local changes, and a morphology branch captures global shape patterns from a two-dimensional waveform view. 
    The fused evidence is then projected into the LLM embedding space through task-aware pretraining, enabling the pretrained model to operate on time-series-derived tokens. 
    For prediction, TimeMorph replaces unconstrained text generation with closed-set class-token scoring over the valid label set, and further incorporates semantic label priors when meaningful class names are available. 
    Experiments on all 128 UCR datasets under full-label-space 1-, 2-, 5-, and 10-shot protocols show that TimeMorph achieves the best average accuracy and average rank among strong task-specific, Transformer-style, and LLM-style baselines. 
    Additional external evaluations and ablation studies further validate the contributions of dual-view modeling, embedding-space alignment, and class-token scoring. 
    These results suggest that pretrained LLMs can serve as effective reusable priors for few-shot TSC when numerical time-series evidence is properly represented, aligned, and decoded.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time series classification (TSC) is challenging when labeled sequences are scarce in healthcare monitoring, industrial diagnosis, sensor analytics, and related domains \citep{ismail2019deep}. In many practical scenarios, collecting labels requires domain expertise, new categories may appear with only a few examples, and class distributions can be highly imbalanced. Reliable few-shot TSC therefore requires more than fitting a classifier on a small support set. A successful model should capture transferable evidence from both temporal dynamics and waveform morphology, reflecting how humans naturally interpret sequences by tracking numerical evolution while recognizing shape-level patterns.

Discriminative evidence in time series is inherently heterogeneous. Some classes are distinguished by chronological dynamics, such as local trends, amplitudes, phase shifts, and long-range evolution, while others depend more strongly on waveform morphology, such as recurring peaks, valleys, cycles, motifs, and discriminative subsequences \citep{tang2020interpretable,ye2009time,dempster2020rocket,fawaz2020inceptiontime,wang2015encoding}. This dual-view observation suggests that effective few-shot TSC models should extract and combine both temporal and morphological features. However, many existing methods emphasize one dominant form of evidence. CNNs and Transformer-based models can achieve strong performance when abundant labeled training data are available, but they may overfit to unstable cues and generalize poorly under few-shot supervision \citep{fawaz2020inceptiontime,dempster2020rocket,dyformer}. Prototype-based methods are more data-efficient, yet they still do not fully resolve the need to jointly preserve temporal and morphological evidence \citep{snell2017prototypical,tapnet,tang2020interpretable}. In particular, models that mainly operate on ordered numerical trends can overlook the intuitive visual structure of the waveform, while purely shape-oriented representations may miss chronological dependencies needed for fine-grained discrimination.

Recently, pretrained large language models (LLMs) have become appealing for few-shot learning because they provide reusable pretrained computation, conditional-generation objectives, and token-level scoring interfaces \citep{brown2020language,raffel2020exploring}. Time-series-specific adaptation work has explored numerical serialization, reprogramming, embedding alignment, and semantic anchors, but many of these methods focus on forecasting, representation learning, or open-ended generation rather than closed-set classification \citep{gruver2023large,zhou2023one,jin2024time,sun2023test,chang2025llm4ts,zhang2024large,jiang2024empowering,langer2025opentslm,tan2024language,zheng2024revisited}. Few-shot time-series classification with LLMs has only recently started to emerge \citep{chen2025large}. These properties make LLMs a promising backbone for few-shot TSC, where supervised labels are limited but transferable computation and flexible decision interfaces are valuable. However, directly applying LLMs to time series remains challenging. Time-series observations are continuous, domain-specific, and structurally different from the discrete token sequences on which language models are pretrained. Moreover, few-shot TSC requires reliable closed-set discrimination over dataset-specific labels.

To make pretrained LLMs effective for dual-view few-shot TSC, three core challenges must be addressed. First, the representation should preserve both chronological dynamics and morphology-oriented cues, since either view may contain the decisive class signal. Second, fused continuous time-series features must be aligned with the pretrained LLM input space before target adaptation. Third, prediction should be restricted to valid class candidates instead of relying on unconstrained text generation, which may produce invalid labels and introduce tokenization or label-length bias \citep{raffel2020exploring,hokamp2017lexically,koo2024automata}. Without such an interface, the language model may become only a loosely connected generative component rather than a reliable few-shot classifier.

We propose \textbf{TimeMorph}, a dual-view time-series-to-language framework for few-shot TSC. For each sequence, TimeMorph constructs two complementary views: a temporal branch that models ordered local relations and long-range dynamics, and a morphology branch that transforms the waveform into a two-dimensional morphology map so that shape-level structure can be extracted by a frozen vision encoder. The two views are fused into continuous tokens and projected into a pretrained causal LLM, reducing the mismatch between time-series representations and the language-model embedding space. For downstream classification, TimeMorph replaces unconstrained decoding with supervised class-token scoring over dataset-specific class tokens, turning the generative interface into a discriminative closed-set classifier. When class names carry meaningful semantics, label verbalizers further initialize and regularize the corresponding class-token embeddings without requiring multi-token phrase decoding.

Our contributions are summarized as follows. First, we formulate LLM-based few-shot TSC as an interface-design problem that jointly considers complementary time-series evidence, continuous-to-language alignment, and closed-set prediction. Second, we introduce a dual-view time-series-to-language encoder that preserves chronological and morphology-oriented evidence before transferring continuous tokens into a pretrained causal LLM. Third, we propose supervised class-token scoring over valid labels, and extend it with label-verbalizer initialization and regularization when semantic label names are available. Across 128 UCR datasets, TimeMorph achieves the best average accuracy and average rank under 1-, 2-, 5-, and 10-shot settings, while additional external experiments and ablation studies validate the roles of dual-view modeling, embedding-space alignment, and closed-set class-token scoring.


\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{LLMs and foundation models for time series.}
Recent surveys show that foundation-model research for time series has expanded from adapting pretrained language models to building native temporal foundation models \cite{zhang2024large,jiang2024empowering,ye2024survey,shi2025large}. Representative adaptation-based methods include TEST \cite{sun2023test}, which aligns time-series embeddings with the LLM embedding space through instance-, feature-, and text-prototype-aware contrastive learning; Time-LLM \cite{jin2024time}, which reprograms time-series patches into a frozen LLM via text prototypes; and LLM4TS \cite{chang2025llm4ts}, which uses a two-stage alignment-and-task fine-tuning strategy. OpenTSLM \cite{langer2025opentslm} moves toward time-series language models that treat time series as a native modality through soft-prompt or cross-attention interfaces. At the same time, recent ablation studies caution that LLM components do not automatically improve time-series performance when the modality interface is poorly designed \cite{tan2024language,zheng2024revisited}. The closest few-shot classification line is LLMFew \cite{chen2025large}, which introduces a patch-wise temporal convolution encoder and LoRA-tuned decoder for few-shot multivariate TSC. In contrast, our work focuses on dual-view temporal evidence, explicit time-series-to-language alignment, and class-token scoring rather than an external classifier head.

\paragraph{Temporal and morphological evidence in TSC.}
Classical and deep TSC methods support the view that discriminative evidence can appear at multiple structural levels. Shapelet methods identify local subsequences with high class-discriminative power \cite{ye2009shapelets,tang2020interpretable}, convolutional methods such as InceptionTime and ROCKET exploit local receptive fields and kernel responses at multiple scales \cite{fawaz2020inceptiontime,dempster2020rocket}, and patch-based Transformers preserve local subseries information while enabling longer-context attention \cite{nietime}. These methods primarily operate in the chronological-dependency space. In parallel, time-series imaging methods transform one-dimensional signals into two-dimensional structures such as Gramian angular fields and Markov transition fields, enabling vision models to capture global shape and transition patterns \cite{wang2015imaging}. Recent work further shows that visualization can improve LLM reasoning over time series and that frozen vision transformers applied to imaged time series can produce strong classification representations \cite{liu2025picture,roschmann2025time}. These two lines motivate our dual-space hypothesis: few-shot TSC labels may depend on both chronological dependencies and morphological structures.

\paragraph{Generative classification and semantic-prior label spaces.}
Casting classification as generation is widely used in text-to-text learning, where labels are generated as target tokens under the same sequence objective as other tasks \cite{raffel2020exploring}. However, unconstrained decoding is mismatched with closed-set classification because many generated strings are irrelevant to the valid label set. Prior work on constrained decoding motivates restricting generation to valid lexical or structural outputs \cite{hokamp2017lexically,koo2024automata}. TimeMorph adopts the same closed-set principle but implements the final decision as supervised class-token scoring: the model learns dataset-specific class-token rows and compares their next-token logits rather than performing unconstrained generation. For datasets with meaningful label names, we further use label verbalizers as semantic priors over the same class-token rows. This keeps the decision aligned with the autoregressive interface while avoiding the length bias, shared-prefix artifacts, and language-prior sensitivity of direct phrase-likelihood classification.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We propose \textbf{TimeMorph}, a dual-view time-series-to-language framework that adapts a pretrained causal language model to few-shot time series classification (TSC).
The central idea is to use a pretrained LLM for numerical time-series data without serializing the raw sequence into text.
Given an input sequence, TimeMorph first extracts complementary temporal and morphology-based evidence, then maps the resulting time-series tokens into the LLM embedding space, and finally performs closed-set classification by scoring dataset-specific class tokens.

\subsection{Problem Setup}
\label{sec:problem-formulation}

\paragraph{Univariate time series classification.}
Let $D$ denote a target classification dataset with label set
$\mathcal{Y}_D=\{0,\ldots,C-1\}$.
Each example consists of a univariate time series $x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$ and a class label $y\in\mathcal{Y}_D$.
Given a query sequence $x$, the goal of TSC is to predict its class label from the temporal signal.
We focus on closed-set classification over the full label space: all $C$ classes are present during adaptation and evaluation, and prediction is restricted to the valid label set $\mathcal{Y}_D$.

\paragraph{Few-shot learning setup.}
In the $K$-shot setting, the support set
$\mathcal{S}_D^{K}$ contains at most $K$ labeled examples per class:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:k-shot-support}
\mathcal{S}_D^{K}
=
\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_D},
\qquad
|\{i:y_i=c\}|\le K,\quad c\in\mathcal{Y}_D .
\end{equation}
The model is trained on $\mathcal{S}_D^{K}$ and evaluated on query examples from the official test split.



\subsection{Framework}
\label{sec:timemorph-framework}

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} gives an overview of TimeMorph.
The architecture consists of three components: a dual-view encoder, an embedding-space projection interface, and a closed-set class-token scoring rule.
The dual-view encoder $G_{\theta}$ extracts complementary evidence from a temporal view and a morphology view encoder.
The projection interface $P_{\psi}$ maps the fused time-series tokens to the hidden dimension of a pretrained causal LLM.
The class-token scoring rule converts the LLM output logits into predictions over the valid labels of the target dataset.
These components are learned with a staged training schedule described in Section~\ref{sec:pretraining-adaptation}.

\subsubsection{Dual-View Time-Series Encoding}
\label{sec:dual-view-encoder}

Time series labels can depend on different types of evidence.
Some classes are distinguished by chronological dynamics, such as local trends, phase changes, amplitude variations, or long-range evolution.
Others are better characterized by waveform morphology, such as peaks, valleys, cycles, motifs, or discriminative subsequences.
TimeMorph therefore represents each input sequence through two complementary views and processes them with two corresponding branches.

\paragraph{Temporal encoder.}
The temporal view preserves the original chronological ordering of the raw sequence.
Let $x^{\mathrm{pad}}$ be the sequence after right replicate padding.
Given patch length $\ell$ and stride $s$, we extract overlapping temporal patches
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:temporal-patches}
R_i=x^{\mathrm{pad}}_{(i-1)s+1:(i-1)s+\ell},
\qquad
 i=1,\ldots,N_t,
\qquad
N_t=\left\lfloor\frac{T+p_t-\ell}{s}\right\rfloor+1,
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is the padding length needed to form complete patches.
Each patch is linearly embedded, combined with a learnable positional embedding, and passed through a temporal Transformer:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:temporal-encoder}
Z^{t}(x)
=
G_t
\left(
\left[
W_tR_1+\pi_1;
\ldots;
W_tR_{N_t}+\pi_{N_t}
\right]
\right).
\end{equation}
The output $Z^t(x)$ is a sequence of temporal tokens that encode ordered patch-level dynamics of the original waveform.

\paragraph{Morphology encoder.}
The morphology view exposes shape-level structure by converting the same one-dimensional sequence into a two-dimensional morphology map.
We first normalize $x$ with robust per-sequence statistics:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-normalization}
\bar{x}_t
=
\frac{x_t-\operatorname{median}(x)}
{\operatorname{IQR}(x)+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
The normalized sequence is then unfolded into overlapping local windows.
Let $q=\max(1,\lfloor\sqrt{T}\rfloor)$ be the window length and
$\delta=\max(1,\lfloor\rho q\rfloor)$ be the stride.
We construct the morphology map by normalizing the unfolded matrix, resizing it to the input resolution of the vision backbone, and replicating it across RGB channels:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:morphology-map}
I(x)
=
\operatorname{RGB}
\left(
\operatorname{Resize}
\left(
\Gamma_{\mathrm{img}}
\left(
\operatorname{Unfold}_{q,\delta}(\bar{x})
\right)
\right)
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{img}}(\cdot)$ denotes per-map normalization and contrast adjustment.
Each row of $I(x)$ corresponds to a local waveform window, so the map preserves local subsequence shapes while making cross-window morphology visible to a vision encoder.
This construction exposes repeated local shapes and cross-window transitions rather than treating the time series as an arbitrary natural image.
A frozen vision backbone $G_m$ extracts morphology tokens:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:morphology-encoder}
Z^{m}(x)=G_m(I(x)).
\end{equation}
We discard the vision class token and use only the patch tokens.
The vision backbone remains frozen; trainable parameters are introduced in the temporal branch, projection modules, class-token rows, and LLM adapters.

\paragraph{Dual-view fusion.}
The temporal and morphology tokens are projected to a common dimension and concatenated:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dual-view-fusion}
\tilde{Z}^{t}(x)=Q_t(Z^{t}(x)),
\qquad
\tilde{Z}^{m}(x)=Q_m(Z^{m}(x)),
\qquad
Z_x=
\left[
\tilde{Z}^{t}(x);
\tilde{Z}^{m}(x)
\right].
\end{equation}
The fused sequence $Z_x\in\mathbb{R}^{N_x\times d_e}$ serves as the time-series token sequence passed to the LLM interface.

\subsubsection{Time-Series Token Projection}
\label{sec:token-projection}

The dual-view encoder produces continuous time-series tokens, whereas a causal language model operates on embeddings of discrete text tokens.
TimeMorph bridges this modality mismatch with an input-side projection interface that maps $Z_x$ into the LLM embedding space, avoiding the need to serialize numerical observations as text.

Let $P_\psi$ be a trainable projection module from encoder dimension $d_e$ to the LLM hidden dimension, $\tau(\cdot)$ be the tokenizer, and $E_{\mathrm{lm}}$ be the LLM input embedding matrix.
For a prompt $p=(p^{\mathrm{pre}},p^{\mathrm{post}})$, the conditioned input embeddings are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditioned-input}
H(x,p)=
\left[
E_{\mathrm{lm}}(\tau(p^{\mathrm{pre}}));
P_{\psi}(Z_x);
E_{\mathrm{lm}}(\tau(p^{\mathrm{post}}))
\right].
\end{equation}
The projected time-series tokens are therefore inserted into the same embedding sequence as text tokens and can condition the causal LLM through its standard forward pass.
When a training or target setting provides auxiliary textual descriptions, they are included as part of $p$ and encoded by the same tokenizer.
The learning objectives for this interface are introduced together with the staged training schedule in Section~\ref{sec:pretraining-adaptation}.

\subsubsection{Closed-Set Class-Token Classification}
\label{sec:class-token-classification}

Few-shot TSC requires prediction over a fixed set of valid labels rather than open-ended text generation.
For each target dataset $D$, TimeMorph assigns a compact set of single-token class identifiers:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-token-set}
\mathcal{C}_D
=
\{
\langle \mathrm{cls}_0\rangle,\ldots,\langle \mathrm{cls}_{C-1}\rangle
\},
\qquad
\mathcal{V}_D
=
\{
v_0,\ldots,v_{C-1}
\},
\end{equation}
where $v_c$ is the vocabulary index of class token $\langle \mathrm{cls}_c\rangle$.
We implement these identifiers as newly added special tokens, so each class corresponds to exactly one vocabulary index.
Let $p_D$ denote the classification prompt for dataset $D$.
After the LLM conditions on $H(x,p_D)$, let $h_{\Theta}(x,p_D)$ be the final hidden state at the last prompt position.
With output embedding matrix $W_{\mathrm{out}}$, the score for class $c$ is the next-token logit assigned to its class token:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-score}
s_c(x)
=
\left[
W_{\mathrm{out}}h_{\Theta}(x,p_D)
\right]_{v_c}.
\end{equation}
Prediction is restricted to the valid class-token set:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-pred}
\hat{y}
=
\arg\max_{c\in\{0,\ldots,C-1\}} s_c(x).
\end{equation}
The supervised objective is the cross entropy over only the valid class tokens:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-token-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ct}}
=
-\log
\frac{\exp s_y(x)}
{\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}\exp s_c(x)}.
\end{equation}
Thus, TimeMorph keeps the autoregressive vocabulary head but turns the final decision into a one-forward-pass closed-set comparison over $C$ valid labels.
Within the vocabulary parameters, only the input embeddings and output rows of the new class tokens are trainable; all other vocabulary rows remain fixed.

\paragraph{Label semantic priors.}
For anonymous-label benchmarks, class tokens are learned as dataset-specific identifiers.
When class names are meaningful, TimeMorph uses label verbalizers to initialize and regularize the same class-token rows.
For class $c$, let $\mathcal{R}_c$ be a set of template-consistent verbalizers derived from its canonical label name.
We compute a semantic prototype by averaging the LLM embeddings of the verbalizers:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:semantic-prototype}
p_c
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_c|}
\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}_c}
\frac{1}{|\tau(r)|}
\sum_{u\in\tau(r)}
E_{\mathrm{lm}}(u).
\end{equation}
The prototype is rescaled to match the average norm of vocabulary rows and is used to initialize the input and output rows of the corresponding class token.
During adaptation, we regularize these rows toward a detached prototype $\tilde{p}_c$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:semantic-row-reg}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sem}}
=
\frac{1}{C}
\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}
\left[
1-\cos(e_{v_c},\tilde{p}_c)
+
1-\cos(w_{v_c},\tilde{p}_c)
\right],
\end{equation}
where $e_{v_c}$ and $w_{v_c}$ are the input and output rows of class token $v_c$.
The adaptation loss is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:adapt-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adapt}}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ct}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{sem}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sem}}.
\end{equation}
For datasets without meaningful label names, we set $\lambda_{\mathrm{sem}}=0$.

\subsubsection{Staged Pretraining and Target Adaptation}
\label{sec:pretraining-adaptation}

TimeMorph learns the time-series-to-language interface with three pretraining stages followed by target-dataset adaptation.
Stage I learns reusable dual-view time-series representations, Stage II aligns projected time-series tokens with the LLM embedding space, and Stage III optionally calibrates the interface with label-related language when semantic supervision is available.
The default UCR benchmark uses the Stage II checkpoint, while the semantic-label external evaluations use the Stage III calibrated checkpoint.

\paragraph{Stage I: Representation Pretraining.}
Stage I learns the dual-view encoder without class labels.
In the default setting, it uses unlabeled raw series from TSQA-train \citep{wang2025chattime}, M4-train \citep{makridakis2020m4}, and official TRAIN-split sequences from a fixed 98-dataset UCR pretraining pool \citep{dau2019ucr}.
No UCR TEST sequence or UCR class label is used in this stage.
We combine masked temporal patch reconstruction with an invariance--variance--covariance objective over augmented views.
For a minibatch $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{B}$ and two augmentations $r\in\{1,2\}$, define pooled representations for branch
$b\in\{t,m,f\}$ as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pooled-batch-representations}
H_b^{(r)}
=
\left[
\operatorname{Pool}_b(Z^b(x_1^{(r)}));
\ldots;
\operatorname{Pool}_b(Z^b(x_B^{(r)}))
\right]
\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times d_b},
\qquad
Z^f(x)=Z_x,
\end{equation}
where $t$, $m$, and $f$ denote the temporal, morphology, and fused branches.
For each branch, the regularization loss is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ivc}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ivc}}^{b}
=
\alpha_{\mathrm{inv}}
\frac{1}{B}
\left\|
H_b^{(1)}-H_b^{(2)}
\right\|_F^2
&+
\beta_{\mathrm{var}}
\sum_{r=1}^{2}\sum_j
\max
\left(
0,
1-\sigma_j(H_b^{(r)})
\right) \\
&+
\gamma_{\mathrm{cov}}
\sum_{r=1}^{2}
\left\|
\operatorname{offdiag}
\left(
\operatorname{Cov}(H_b^{(r)})
\right)
\right\|_F^2 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The three terms respectively match representations of two augmented views, prevent dimension-wise collapse, and penalize redundant covariance
\citep{bardes2022vicreg}.

For reconstruction, a contiguous segment is masked and the temporal branch is asked to recover the original temporal patches:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:masked-rec}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
=
\frac{1}{2}
\sum_{r=1}^{2}
\left\|
R_{\omega}
\left(
Z^{t}(\mathcal{M}(x^{(r)}))
\right)
-
\mathcal{P}_{\ell,s}(x^{(r)})
\right\|_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{M}$ is the masking operator,
$\mathcal{P}_{\ell,s}$ extracts temporal patches using the same patch length and stride as Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal-patches}, and $R_{\omega}$ is the reconstruction head.
The representation-pretraining objective is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:representation-pretraining-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rep}}
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
+
\lambda_t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ivc}}^{t}
+
\lambda_m\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ivc}}^{m}
+
\lambda_f\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ivc}}^{f}.
\end{equation}
We apply branch dropout during this stage to prevent the fused representation from collapsing onto a single view.

\paragraph{Stage II: Embedding-Space Alignment Pretraining.}
Stage II attaches the projector $P_\psi$ and the pretrained causal LLM to the Stage-I dual-view encoder.
Let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{TSQA}}$ denote the TSQA training split from ChatTime \citep{wang2025chattime}.
Each example is represented as $(x,p,a)$, where $x$ is a raw time series, $p$ is a natural-language or template-based question about the series, and $a$ is the textual answer.
The projected tokens $P_\psi(Z_x)$ are inserted into the prompt embedding sequence as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:conditioned-input}.
For answer token sequence $u_{1:M}=\tau(a)$, we apply the language-modeling loss only to the answer tokens:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:answer-lm}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}(a\mid x,p)
=
-\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\log p_{\Theta}
\left(
 u_m
 \mid
 H(x,p), E_{\mathrm{lm}}(u_{<m})
\right).
\end{equation}
The Stage-II objective is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:language-alignment-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{align}}
=
\mathbb{E}_{(x,p,a)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{TSQA}}}
\left[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}(a\mid x,p)
\right].
\end{equation}
This stage does not train TimeMorph to classify any target dataset.
Instead, it teaches the projection interface to produce continuous tokens that the frozen LLM can condition on under its standard next-token objective.
Following the implementation, we first update the projector with the dual-view encoder fixed and then jointly optimize the encoder and projector.
The LLM backbone remains frozen throughout Stage II.
The resulting checkpoint initializes the default UCR target adaptation.

\paragraph{Stage III: Optional Label-Semantic Calibration.}
Stage III is used when the target evaluation contains meaningful label names or textual label descriptions.
It further calibrates the Stage-II interface toward label-related language using a mixture of TSQA retention examples, M4 captioning examples, and synthetic attribute supervision derived from TSQA and M4 raw series \citep{wang2025chattime,makridakis2020m4}.
In the implementation, this corresponds to the semantic-bridge and caption-specialization training after Stage II.
The objective uses the same answer-token language-modeling form as Eq.~\eqref{eq:answer-lm}, with auxiliary caption or attribute targets such as trends, periodicity, spikes, abrupt changes, and other interpretable temporal properties.
Stage III is not used for the anonymous-label UCR benchmark.
For semantic-label external datasets such as MIT-BIH, SleepEDF, and CinC2017 \citep{moody2001impact,kemp2000analysis,clifford2017af}, target adaptation is initialized from the Stage III calibrated checkpoint and uses the label-semantic class-token prior in Eq.~\eqref{eq:semantic-row-reg}.

\paragraph{Target-Dataset Adaptation.}
For each target dataset $D$, adaptation proceeds in two phases.
In the warm-up phase, TimeMorph updates the dual-view encoder, the projection module, and the dataset-specific class-token rows:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:warmup-params}
\Theta_{\mathrm{warm}}
=
\{
\theta,\psi,e_{\mathcal{V}_D},w_{\mathcal{V}_D}
\},
\qquad
\min_{\Theta_{\mathrm{warm}}}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adapt}} .
\end{equation}
This phase calibrates the projected time-series tokens and the closed-set decision space before updating any LLM adapters.

In the joint adaptation phase, low-rank adapters are added to the LLM:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lora-params}
\Theta_{\mathrm{joint}}
=
\Theta_{\mathrm{warm}}
\cup
\{
\Delta\Theta_{\mathrm{LoRA}}
\},
\qquad
\Delta W_l=B_lA_l,
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}(\Delta W_l)\le r.
\end{equation}
The pretrained LLM weights remain frozen, and only adapter parameters are updated on the LLM side.
At inference time, TimeMorph performs a single forward pass for each query sequence and ranks the valid class tokens using Eq.~\eqref{eq:class-score}.
Implementation details and staged-training data sources are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on all 128 datasets in the UCR Archive 2018 with a fixed full-label-space few-shot protocol. For each dataset $D$ and $K\in\{1,2,5,10\}$, all classes are included; we sample up to $K$ labeled examples per class from the official training split and evaluate on all examples in the official test split. All methods use the same sampled support sets within each of five independent runs.

\paragraph{Pretraining data and default initialization.}
The main UCR benchmark uses the Stage II checkpoint: Stage I is trained on raw TSQA-train series, raw M4-train series, and unlabeled official TRAIN-split sequences from a fixed 98-dataset UCR pool; Stage II is trained on the TSQA training split. Stage III is not used for Table~\ref{tab:ucr_fewshot_paper_current-fewshot-main}. No pretraining stage uses any official UCR TEST split or UCR class label. For UCR datasets included in the 98-dataset pool, TimeMorph may have seen unlabeled official TRAIN sequences before target adaptation; after this pretraining, each target run uses only the sampled support labels for supervised adaptation. For the external semantic-label datasets, target adaptation uses the Stage III calibrated checkpoint.


\paragraph{Baselines and metrics.}
We compare against CNN/prototype baselines (\textsc{ResNet}, \textsc{TapNet}, \textsc{COSCO-ResNet}), Transformer-style or decomposition-based baselines (\textsc{PatchTST}, \textsc{DLinear}, \textsc{TimesNet}, \textsc{Autoformer}, \textsc{Crossformer}, \textsc{FEDformer}, \textsc{Informer}), and the LLM-style \textsc{GPT4TS} baseline. Accuracy is averaged over five runs and then macro-averaged over datasets. \texttt{Avg} averages the four shot settings, and \texttt{AvgRank} averages dataset-level ranks across shots; lower rank is better. Standard deviations and full per-dataset tables are in Appendix~\ref{app:full-ucr-results}.

\paragraph{Fairness and training details.}
All methods share the same official TRAIN support pool, TEST query split, and sampled support indices. We do not add a validation split by default; each method follows its runner's checkpoint-selection rule, and some baselines select checkpoints using support-set performance. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline-details} lists the training recipes and hyperparameters.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
TimeMorph uses \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.2-1B}, \texttt{facebook/dinov2-base}, temporal patch length 16, stride 8, hidden size 128, and the Stage II checkpoint for UCR. Target adaptation uses 5 warm-up epochs, 55 joint-training epochs, and LoRA with rank 16 and alpha 32. Additional details are in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\subsection{Main Results}

\paragraph{Benchmark-level comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:ucr_fewshot_paper_current-fewshot-main} shows that TimeMorph obtains the highest mean accuracy under all four $K$-shot settings: 48.31, 55.55, 64.14, and 69.16 for 1-, 2-, 5-, and 10-shot. The strongest non-TimeMorph baseline is \textsc{COSCO-ResNet}, with 47.88, 54.95, 63.55, and 67.18. Thus the improvement over the strongest specialized CNN/prototype baseline is modest in accuracy, especially in the lowest-shot regimes, but it is consistent across $K$ and is also reflected in the best \texttt{AvgRank} (4.24 vs. 4.70 for \textsc{COSCO-ResNet}). The gap is more substantial against Transformer-style and LLM-style baselines: TimeMorph improves \texttt{Avg} by 4.53 points over \textsc{Informer} and 18.36 points over \textsc{GPT4TS}. These results suggest that the proposed interface is most clearly beneficial relative to generic sequence and LLM-style baselines, while remaining competitive with strong specialized CNN/prototype methods.

\subsection{Ablation Studies}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-summary} summarizes the main top-level UCR ablations on all 128 datasets. Each ablation follows the same five-run full-label-space few-shot protocol and modifies only the component under study. Focused compact views with the corresponding drops are provided in Tables~\ref{tab:ablation-pretrain}--\ref{tab:ablation-decoding}. Because UCR labels are anonymous, the decision ablation tests closed-set class-token scoring rather than semantic label priors. The no-LLM ablation is reported separately in Table~\ref{tab:ablation-llm-backbone}, with the full per-dataset comparison in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ablation-llm-backbone-appendix}.

\input{tables/main/ucr_fewshot_ablation_summary.tex}

\paragraph{Effect of staged pretraining.}
Removing staged pretraining drops \texttt{Avg} from 59.29 to 55.94, with shot-level accuracies decreasing from 48.31/55.55/64.14/69.16 to 44.70/51.81/60.65/66.60 for 1-/2-/5-/10-shot, respectively. The focused view in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ablation-pretrain} reports a 3.35-point average drop, suggesting that the aggregate Stage I+II initialization helps when target supervision is too limited to learn the representation and LLM interface from scratch. This ablation removes the full pretraining pipeline at once and should not be read as isolating each substage.

\paragraph{Effect of dual-view time-series-to-language modeling.}
Temporal-only and morphology-only variants reach \texttt{Avg} scores of 54.21 and 55.09, below the full model's 59.29. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ablation-dual-view} shows that removing the morphology branch yields a 5.08-point average drop, while removing the temporal branch yields a 4.20-point drop. The temporal-only variant is weaker at 1-, 2-, and 5-shot, whereas the morphology-only variant is weaker at 2- and 10-shot. Neither single view dominates across all shots, which supports the intended complementarity between chronological and morphological evidence.

\paragraph{Effect of class-token scoring.}
Disabling class-token scoring reduces \texttt{Avg} from 59.29 to 55.14. As detailed in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ablation-decoding}, the corresponding shot-level accuracies decrease from 48.31/55.55/64.14/69.16 to 44.46/50.44/60.08/65.59, giving a 4.15-point average drop. This indicates that, under the same adaptation budget, unconstrained generative adaptation is less stable for closed-set few-shot classification than directly scoring the dataset-specific label tokens.

\paragraph{Effect of semantic label priors.}
For external datasets with meaningful label names, Table~\ref{tab:semantic-prior-summary} compares anonymous class-token rows with rows initialized and regularized from label-verbalizer prototypes, using the Stage III calibrated checkpoint for both variants. Semantic priors improve the 10/20/30-shot average on MIT-BIH, SleepEDF, and CinC2017, increasing the overall average from 47.61 to 50.43. The full appendix table shows that the benefit is not uniform at every shot, with a decrease on CinC2017 20-shot, but the averaged result supports the semantic-prior class-token space as a useful extension of supervised closed-set decoding.

\input{tables/main/semantic_prior_summary.tex}

\paragraph{Effect of the LLM backbone.}
The no-LLM variant replaces the language pathway with a lightweight linear classifier on the same dual-view features. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:ablation-llm-backbone}, this ablation is evaluated on all 128 UCR datasets under the same five-run protocol. Removing the LLM backbone reduces \texttt{Avg} from 59.29 to 51.46, a 7.83-point drop. The decrease grows with the number of support examples: accuracy changes from 48.31 to 44.89 at 1-shot, 55.55 to 48.29 at 2-shot, 64.14 to 54.22 at 5-shot, and 69.16 to 58.43 at 10-shot. This suggests that, under our implementation and adaptation schedule, the language pathway contributes more than a lightweight classifier on the same features; however, this ablation does not isolate whether the gain comes from pretrained knowledge, additional capacity, or the token-scoring interface.

Overall, the ablations support the central design: staged pretraining improves transferability, dual-view modeling preserves more complete evidence, closed-set class-token scoring stabilizes the final decision, and semantic-prior row initialization and regularization further improve semantic-label transfer.

\section{Limitations}

TimeMorph improves average accuracy and average rank, but its gains over the strongest CNN/prototype baselines are modest in extremely low-shot settings.
The main empirical message is therefore not that LLM-based TSC now universally dominates specialized CNN-style few-shot classifiers, but that a carefully designed time-series-to-language interface can substantially outperform previous Transformer-style and LLM-style baselines while remaining competitive with strong task-specific methods.
TimeMorph also has a larger inference footprint than compact CNN baselines because it reuses a pretrained causal LLM, even though only a small fraction of parameters is updated during target adaptation.
In our efficiency probe, this makes TimeMorph two to three orders of magnitude slower at inference than compact CNN/prototype baselines.
The current UCR pretraining setting also uses unlabeled official TRAIN sequences from a fixed subset of UCR datasets, so it does not establish strict leave-dataset-out transfer for every UCR target dataset.
The external semantic-prior results improve the averaged performance on all three semantic-label datasets, but individual shot-level effects still vary, so label semantics should be viewed as a prior over the class-token rows rather than as a replacement for supervised adaptation.
TimeMorph may benefit applications where labeled time-series data are scarce, such as physiological monitoring or industrial inspection, but the present study is not a deployment-ready clinical or safety-critical system.
Any deployment in such domains would require domain-specific validation, privacy review, and failure analysis beyond the scope of this paper.

\paragraph{Morphology-map transform.}
The operator $\Gamma_{\mathrm{img}}$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:morphology-map} applies per-sample min--max normalization over the two-dimensional grid and a fixed contrast adjustment $u\mapsto u^{0.8}$. In the default morphology mode, the effective window length is $\max(1,\lfloor\sqrt{T}\rfloor)$, the stride length is $\max(1,\lfloor 0.1q\rfloor)$, incomplete windows are completed by replicate padding on the left, and the resulting grid is resized to the DINOv2 image resolution with nearest-neighbor interpolation before RGB replication. We use DINOv2 patch tokens from the configured intermediate layer and exclude the class token.

\paragraph{Stage I pretraining hyperparameters.}
Stage I uses mask ratio $0.4$, branch-dropout probability $0.1$, jitter standard deviation $0.03$, scaling range $[0.8,1.2]$, and time-mask ratio $0.1$ for the two augmented views. The Stage I loss weights are $\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}=1.0$, $\lambda_t=0.25$, $\lambda_v=0.25$, and $\lambda_f=0.5$. These values are fixed before the target few-shot runs.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency Probe Details}
\label{app:efficiency-details}

The efficiency probe is implemented by the repository's \texttt{efficiency\_probe.py} script.
It uses the same model families as the main benchmark and omits TimeMorph-only ablations.
The default runtime subset contains \textsc{Coffee}, \textsc{GunPoint}, \textsc{ECG5000}, \textsc{ElectricDevices}, \textsc{Crop}, \textsc{ACSF1}, \textsc{Phoneme}, and \textsc{NonInvasiveFetalECGThorax1}, covering short, medium, and long UCR series as well as different class counts.
The script writes the LaTeX tables, CSV summary, and accuracy-versus-updated-parameters plot to the efficiency-report directory.
