\begin{abstract}
  Few-shot time series classification (TSC) is challenging due to scarce labeled sequences. Reliable predictions require capturing both temporal dynamics and waveform morphology, reflecting how humans interpret sequential data by tracking numeric trends and recognizing overall shape patterns. Pretrained LLMs offer few-shot generative interfaces, but directly applying them is challenging: continuous time-series features are not naturally aligned with language embeddings, and predictions must be restricted to a fixed set of target classes. Therefore, we propose TimeMorph, a dual-view framework that extracts temporal dynamics via a temporal branch and waveform morphology via a morphology branch. To bridge time-series features and language embeddings, we employ stage-wise pretraining to project the fused tokens into a pretrained causal LLM. Extensive few-shot experiments on the UCR benchmark and additional external datasets show that TimeMorph consistently outperforms strong task-specific, Transformer-style, and LLM-style baselines. Further analyses validate the contributions of dual-view modeling, stage-wise pretraining, and class-token scoring.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time series classification (TSC) is challenging when labeled sequences are scarce in healthcare monitoring, industrial diagnosis, sensor analytics, and related domains \citep{ismail2019deep}. In many practical scenarios, collecting labels requires domain expertise, new categories may appear with only a few examples, and class distributions can be highly imbalanced. Reliable few-shot TSC therefore requires more than fitting a classifier on a small support set. A successful model should capture transferable evidence from both temporal dynamics and waveform morphology, reflecting how humans naturally interpret sequences by tracking numerical evolution while recognizing shape-level patterns.

Discriminative evidence in time series is inherently heterogeneous. Some classes are distinguished by chronological dynamics, such as local trends, amplitudes, phase shifts, and long-range evolution, while others depend more strongly on waveform morphology, such as recurring peaks, valleys, cycles, motifs, and discriminative subsequences \citep{tang2020interpretable,ye2009time,dempster2020rocket,fawaz2020inceptiontime,wang2015encoding}. This dual-view observation suggests that effective few-shot TSC models should extract and combine both temporal and morphological features. However, many existing methods emphasize one dominant form of evidence. CNNs and Transformer-based models can achieve strong performance when abundant labeled training data are available, but they may overfit to unstable cues and generalize poorly under few-shot supervision \citep{fawaz2020inceptiontime,dempster2020rocket,dyformer}. Prototype-based methods are more data-efficient, yet they still do not fully resolve the need to jointly preserve temporal and morphological evidence \citep{snell2017prototypical,tapnet,tang2020interpretable}. In particular, models that mainly operate on ordered numerical trends can overlook the intuitive visual structure of the waveform, while purely shape-oriented representations may miss chronological dependencies needed for fine-grained discrimination.

Recently, pretrained large language models (LLMs) have become appealing for few-shot learning because they provide reusable pretrained computation, conditional-generation objectives, and token-level scoring interfaces \citep{brown2020language,raffel2020exploring}. Time-series-specific adaptation work has explored numerical serialization, reprogramming, embedding alignment, and semantic anchors, but many of these methods focus on forecasting, representation learning, or open-ended generation rather than closed-set classification \citep{gruver2023large,zhou2023one,jin2024time,sun2023test,chang2025llm4ts,zhang2024large,jiang2024empowering,langer2025opentslm,tan2024language,zheng2024revisited}. Few-shot time-series classification with LLMs has only recently started to emerge \citep{chen2025large}. These properties make LLMs a promising backbone for few-shot TSC, where supervised labels are limited but transferable computation and flexible decision interfaces are valuable. However, directly applying LLMs to time series remains challenging. Time-series observations are continuous, domain-specific, and structurally different from the discrete token sequences on which language models are pretrained. Moreover, few-shot TSC requires reliable closed-set discrimination over dataset-specific labels.

To make pretrained LLMs effective for dual-view few-shot TSC, three core challenges must be addressed. First, the representation should preserve both chronological dynamics and morphology-oriented cues, since either view may contain the decisive class signal. Second, fused continuous time-series features must be aligned with the pretrained LLM input space before downstream adaptation. Third, prediction should be restricted to valid class candidates instead of relying on unconstrained text generation, which may produce invalid labels and introduce tokenization or label-length bias \citep{raffel2020exploring,hokamp2017lexically,koo2024automata}. Without such an interface, the language model may become only a loosely connected generative component rather than a reliable few-shot classifier.

We propose \textbf{TimeMorph}, a dual-view time-series-to-language framework for few-shot TSC. For each sequence, TimeMorph constructs two complementary views: a temporal branch that models ordered local relations and long-range dynamics, and a morphology branch that transforms the waveform into a two-dimensional morphology map so that shape-level structure can be extracted by a frozen vision encoder. The two views are fused into continuous tokens and projected into a pretrained causal LLM through stage-wise pretraining, reducing the mismatch between time-series representations and the language-model embedding space. For downstream classification, TimeMorph replaces unconstrained decoding with supervised class-token scoring over dataset-specific class tokens, turning the generative interface into a discriminative closed-set classifier. When class names carry meaningful semantics, label verbalizers further initialize and regularize the corresponding class-token embeddings without requiring multi-token phrase decoding.

Our contributions are summarized as follows. First, we formulate LLM-based few-shot TSC as an interface-design problem that jointly considers complementary time-series evidence, continuous-to-language alignment, and closed-set prediction. Second, we introduce a dual-view time-series-to-language encoder that preserves chronological and morphology-oriented evidence before transferring continuous tokens into a pretrained causal LLM through stage-wise pretraining. Third, we propose supervised class-token scoring over valid labels, and extend it with label-verbalizer initialization and regularization when semantic label names are available. Across 128 UCR datasets, TimeMorph achieves the best average accuracy and average rank under 1-, 2-, 5-, and 10-shot settings, while additional external experiments and ablation studies validate the roles of dual-view modeling, stage-wise pretraining, and closed-set class-token scoring.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{LLMs and foundation models for time series.}
Recent surveys show that foundation-model research for time series has expanded from adapting pretrained language models to building native temporal foundation models \cite{zhang2024large,jiang2024empowering,ye2024survey,shi2025large}. Representative adaptation-based methods include TEST \cite{sun2023test}, which aligns time-series embeddings with the LLM embedding space through instance-, feature-, and text-prototype-aware contrastive learning; Time-LLM \cite{jin2024time}, which reprograms time-series patches into a frozen LLM via text prototypes; and LLM4TS \cite{chang2025llm4ts}, which uses a two-stage alignment-and-task fine-tuning strategy. OpenTSLM \cite{langer2025opentslm} moves toward time-series language models that treat time series as a native modality through soft-prompt or cross-attention interfaces. At the same time, recent ablation studies caution that LLM components do not automatically improve time-series performance when the modality interface is poorly designed \cite{tan2024language,zheng2024revisited}. The closest few-shot classification line is LLMFew \cite{chen2025large}, which introduces a patch-wise temporal convolution encoder and LoRA-tuned decoder for few-shot multivariate TSC. In contrast, our work focuses on dual-view temporal evidence, explicit time-series-to-language alignment, and class-token scoring rather than an external classifier head.

\paragraph{Temporal and morphological evidence in TSC.}
Classical and deep TSC methods support the view that discriminative evidence can appear at multiple structural levels. Shapelet methods identify local subsequences with high class-discriminative power \cite{ye2009shapelets,tang2020interpretable}, convolutional methods such as InceptionTime and ROCKET exploit local receptive fields and kernel responses at multiple scales \cite{fawaz2020inceptiontime,dempster2020rocket}, and patch-based Transformers preserve local subseries information while enabling longer-context attention \cite{nietime}. These methods primarily operate in the chronological-dependency space. In parallel, time-series imaging methods transform one-dimensional signals into two-dimensional structures such as Gramian angular fields and Markov transition fields, enabling vision models to capture global shape and transition patterns \cite{wang2015imaging}. Recent work further shows that visualization can improve LLM reasoning over time series and that frozen vision transformers applied to imaged time series can produce strong classification representations \cite{liu2025picture,roschmann2025time}. These two lines motivate our dual-space hypothesis: few-shot TSC labels may depend on both chronological dependencies and morphological structures.

\paragraph{Generative classification and semantic-prior label spaces.}
Casting classification as generation is widely used in text-to-text learning, where labels are generated as target tokens under the same sequence objective as other tasks \cite{raffel2020exploring}. However, unconstrained decoding is mismatched with closed-set classification because many generated strings are irrelevant to the valid label set. Prior work on constrained decoding motivates restricting generation to valid lexical or structural outputs \cite{hokamp2017lexically,koo2024automata}. TimeMorph adopts the same closed-set principle but implements the final decision as supervised class-token scoring: the model learns dataset-specific class-token rows and compares their next-token logits rather than performing unconstrained generation. For datasets with meaningful label names, we further use label verbalizers as semantic priors over the same class-token rows. This keeps the decision aligned with the autoregressive interface while avoiding the length bias, shared-prefix artifacts, and language-prior sensitivity of direct phrase-likelihood classification.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

We propose \textbf{TimeMorph}, a dual-view time-series-to-language framework that adapts a pretrained causal language model to few-shot time series classification. The key design is to avoid translating a time series into raw text. Instead, TimeMorph transforms each sequence into a small set of continuous tokens, places these tokens in the language-model embedding stream, and performs classification with class-token scoring over dataset-specific labels. This gives the language model a closed-set decision interface while preserving the temporal and morphological evidence needed for time series classification.

\subsection{Problem Setup and Architecture}
\label{sec:method-overview}

Let $D$ be a target classification dataset with label set
$\mathcal{Y}_D=\{0,\ldots,C-1\}$. In the $S$-shot setting, the support set
$\mathcal{S}_D$ contains at most $S$ labeled sequences per class. Given a query
sequence $x\in\mathbb{R}^{T}$, the goal is to predict its label
$y\in\mathcal{Y}_D$ after adapting to $\mathcal{S}_D$.

TimeMorph separates this problem into three modules, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:overview}. First, a dual-view encoder $G_\theta$ maps the input
sequence into time-series tokens by combining a chronological temporal view and a
morphology-oriented view. Second, a projection module $P_\psi$ maps these
tokens to the input dimension of a pretrained causal language model. Third, a
closed-set class-token scoring interface converts the language model's next-token logits
into a classifier over $\mathcal{Y}_D$. The language-model backbone provides the
shared conditional computation, while dataset-specific class-token rows and
lightweight adapters supply the final decision rule for each target dataset.

\subsection{Dual-View Time-Series Encoder}
\label{sec:dual-view-encoder}

Time series labels can depend on different types of evidence. Some classes are
separated by ordered temporal dynamics, such as local trends, phase changes, or
long-range evolution. Others are better characterized by waveform morphology,
such as peaks, valleys, motifs, cycles, or discriminative subsequences. TimeMorph
therefore constructs two complementary token sequences before interacting with
the language model.

\paragraph{Temporal view.}
The temporal branch preserves the chronological structure of the raw sequence.
Let $x^{\mathrm{pad}}$ be the sequence after right replicate padding. Given patch
length $\ell$ and stride $s$, we extract overlapping temporal patches
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:temporal-patches}
R_i=x^{\mathrm{pad}}_{(i-1)s+1:(i-1)s+\ell},
\qquad
 i=1,\ldots,N_t,
\qquad
N_t=\left\lfloor\frac{T+p_t-\ell}{s}\right\rfloor+1,
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is the padding length needed to form complete patches. Each patch is
linearly embedded, combined with a learnable positional embedding, and passed
through a temporal Transformer:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:temporal-encoder}
Z^{t}(x)
=
G_t
\left(
\left[
W_tR_1+\pi_1;
\ldots;
W_tR_{N_t}+\pi_{N_t}
\right]
\right).
\end{equation}
The output $Z^t(x)$ is a sequence of temporal tokens that encode ordered
patch-level dynamics of the original waveform.

\paragraph{Morphology view.}
The morphology branch exposes shape-level structure by converting the same
one-dimensional sequence into a two-dimensional windowed map. We first normalize
$x$ with robust per-sequence statistics:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-normalization}
\bar{x}_t
=
\frac{x_t-\operatorname{median}(x)}
{\operatorname{IQR}(x)+\epsilon}.
\end{equation}
The normalized sequence is then unfolded into overlapping local windows. Let
$q=\max(1,\lfloor\sqrt{T}\rfloor)$ be the window length and
$\delta=\max(1,\lfloor\rho q\rfloor)$ be the stride. We construct the morphology
map by normalizing the unfolded matrix, resizing it to the input resolution of
the vision backbone, and replicating it across RGB channels:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:morphology-map}
I(x)
=
\operatorname{RGB}
\left(
\operatorname{Resize}
\left(
\Gamma_{\mathrm{img}}
\left(
\operatorname{Unfold}_{q,\delta}(\bar{x})
\right)
\right)
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{img}}(\cdot)$ denotes per-map normalization and contrast
adjustment. Each row of $I(x)$ corresponds to a local waveform window, so the map
preserves local subsequence shapes while making cross-window morphology visible
to a vision encoder. A frozen vision backbone $G_m$ extracts morphology tokens:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:morphology-encoder}
Z^{m}(x)=G_m(I(x)).
\end{equation}
We discard the vision class token and use only the patch tokens. The vision
backbone remains frozen; trainable parameters are introduced in the time-series
encoder, projection modules, class-token rows, and language-side adapters.

\paragraph{Fusion.}
The temporal and morphology tokens are projected to a common dimension and
concatenated:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dual-view-fusion}
\tilde{Z}^{t}(x)=Q_t(Z^{t}(x)),
\qquad
\tilde{Z}^{m}(x)=Q_m(Z^{m}(x)),
\qquad
Z_x=
\left[
\tilde{Z}^{t}(x);
\tilde{Z}^{m}(x)
\right].
\end{equation}
The fused sequence $Z_x\in\mathbb{R}^{N_x\times d_e}$ is the time-series token
sequence used by the language-model interface.

\subsection{Time-Series-to-Language Interface}
\label{sec:ts-token-conditioning}

The dual-view encoder produces continuous time-series tokens, whereas a causal
language model expects embeddings drawn from its token embedding space. TimeMorph
bridges this mismatch by reprogramming $Z_x$ into the language-model embedding
stream rather than serializing the numeric sequence as text.

Let $P_\psi$ be the projector from encoder dimension $d_e$ to the language-model
hidden dimension, $\tau(\cdot)$ be the tokenizer, and $E_{\mathrm{lm}}$ be the
language-model input embedding matrix. For a prompt
$p=(p^{\mathrm{pre}},p^{\mathrm{post}})$, the conditioned input embeddings are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:conditioned-input}
H(x,p)=
\left[
E_{\mathrm{lm}}(\tau(p^{\mathrm{pre}}));
P_{\psi}(Z_x);
E_{\mathrm{lm}}(\tau(p^{\mathrm{post}}))
\right].
\end{equation}
The projected time-series tokens therefore act as continuous prefix tokens inside
the same embedding stream as text. Optional textual descriptions, when available,
are appended to the prompt and encoded by the same tokenizer.

During stage-wise pretraining, the model is optimized on time-series-conditioned
generation examples. For an answer string $a$ with token sequence
$u_{1:M}=\tau(a)$, we apply the language-modeling loss only to the answer tokens:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:answer-lm}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}(a\mid x,p)
=
-\sum_{m=1}^{M}
\log p_{\Theta}
\left(
 u_m
 \mid
 H(x,p), E_{\mathrm{lm}}(u_{<m})
\right).
\end{equation}
This stage calibrates the continuous time-series tokens to the language-model
input space. Downstream few-shot classification uses the same conditioned
representation, but replaces free-form decoding with the closed-set rule below.

\subsection{Closed-Set Class-Token Prediction}
\label{sec:class-token-classification}

Few-shot TSC requires choosing among a fixed set of valid labels. Directly
decoding natural-language label strings is undesirable because generated text may
leave the label set and because phrase likelihoods are affected by tokenization,
label length, and shared prefixes. TimeMorph instead assigns each target dataset
a compact set of single-token class identifiers:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-token-set}
\mathcal{C}_D
=
\{
\langle c_0\rangle,\ldots,\langle c_{C-1}\rangle
\},
\qquad
\mathcal{V}_D
=
\{
v_0,\ldots,v_{C-1}
\},
\end{equation}
where $v_c$ is the vocabulary index of class token $\langle c_c\rangle$.

Let $p_D$ denote the classification prompt for dataset $D$. After the language
model conditions on $H(x,p_D)$, let $h_{\Theta}(x,p_D)$ be the final hidden state
at the last prompt position. With output embedding matrix $W_{\mathrm{out}}$, the
score for class $c$ is the next-token logit assigned to its class token:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-score}
s_c(x)
=
\left[
W_{\mathrm{out}}h_{\Theta}(x,p_D)
\right]_{v_c}.
\end{equation}
Prediction is restricted to the valid class-token set:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-pred}
\hat{y}
=
\arg\max_{c\in\{0,\ldots,C-1\}} s_c(x).
\end{equation}
The supervised objective is the cross entropy over only the valid class tokens:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:class-token-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ct}}
=
-\log
\frac{\exp s_y(x)}
{\sum_{c=0}^{C-1}\exp s_c(x)}.
\end{equation}
Thus, the autoregressive vocabulary head is retained, but the decision is made by
one forward pass and one restricted comparison over $C$ valid class tokens.
Within the vocabulary parameters, only the input embeddings and output rows of
the new class tokens are trainable; all other vocabulary rows remain fixed.

\paragraph{Semantic initialization for named labels.}
For anonymous-label benchmarks, class tokens are learned as dataset-specific
identifiers. When class names are meaningful, TimeMorph initializes each
class-token row from the average embedding of template-consistent label
verbalizers and regularizes its input and output rows toward this semantic
prototype during adaptation. The resulting loss is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:adapt-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{adapt}}
=
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ct}}
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{sem}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sem}}.
\end{equation}
For datasets without meaningful label names, we set $\lambda_{\mathrm{sem}}=0$;
prototype construction and row regularization details are in
Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\subsection{Stage-wise Pretraining and Few-Shot Adaptation}
\label{sec:stage-wise-transfer}

TimeMorph is trained in stages. Stage I learns time-series representations
without class labels by combining masked temporal patch reconstruction with an
invariance--variance--covariance regularizer \citep{bardes2022vicreg} over the
temporal, morphology, and fused streams:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stage1-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{I}}
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}
+
\lambda_t\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ivc}}^{t}
+
\lambda_m\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ivc}}^{m}
+
\lambda_f\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ivc}}^{f}.
\end{equation}
We also use branch dropout to prevent the fused representation from collapsing
onto a single view. Stage II attaches $P_\psi$ and the frozen causal language
model, then calibrates the interface with an answer-token language-modeling loss
on time-series question-answering examples:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stage2-loss}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{II}}
=
\mathbb{E}_{(x,p,a)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{TSQA}}}
\left[
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{LM}}(a\mid x,p)
\right].
\end{equation}
We first update the projector with the time-series encoder fixed, and then jointly
optimize the encoder and projector; the language-model backbone remains frozen.
An optional semantic stage further aligns outputs with label-related language
when meaningful class names or descriptions are available, but it is not used for
the anonymous-label UCR benchmark.

For each downstream dataset, adaptation first updates the dual-view encoder, the
projection module, and the dataset-specific class-token rows. A second phase
additionally updates LoRA adapters while keeping the pretrained language-model
weights frozen. At inference time, TimeMorph performs one forward pass per query
and ranks only the valid class tokens using Eq.~\eqref{eq:class-score}.
Implementation details and stage-wise data sources are provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate on all 128 datasets in the UCR Archive 2018 with a fixed full-label-space few-shot protocol. For each dataset $D$ and support size $S\in\{1,2,5,10\}$, all classes are included; we sample up to $S$ labeled examples per class from the official training split and evaluate on all examples in the official test split. All methods use the same sampled support sets within each of five independent runs.

\paragraph{Pretraining data and default initialization.}
The main benchmark uses the Stage II checkpoint: Stage I is trained on raw TSQA-train series, raw M4-train series, and unlabeled official TRAIN-split sequences from a fixed 98-dataset UCR pool; Stage II is trained on the TSQA training split. Stage III is not used for Table~\ref{tab:ucr_fewshot_paper_current-fewshot-main}. No pretraining stage uses any official UCR TEST split or UCR class label. For UCR datasets included in the 98-dataset pool, TimeMorph may have seen unlabeled official TRAIN sequences before downstream adaptation; after this pretraining, each target run uses only the sampled support labels for supervised adaptation.


\paragraph{Baselines and metrics.}
We compare against CNN/prototype baselines (\textsc{ResNet}, \textsc{TapNet}, \textsc{COSCO-ResNet}), Transformer-style or decomposition-based baselines (\textsc{PatchTST}, \textsc{DLinear}, \textsc{TimesNet}, \textsc{Autoformer}, \textsc{Crossformer}, \textsc{FEDformer}, \textsc{Informer}), and the LLM-style \textsc{GPT4TS} baseline. Accuracy is averaged over five independent support-set draws and then macro-averaged over datasets. \texttt{Avg} averages the four shot settings, and \texttt{AvgRank} averages dataset-level ranks across shots; lower rank is better. Appendix tables report the sample standard deviation over these runs; we do not assume normality or perform formal hypothesis testing.

\paragraph{Fairness and training details.}
All methods share the same official TRAIN support pool, TEST query split, and sampled support indices. We do not add a validation split by default; each method follows its runner's checkpoint-selection rule, and some baselines select checkpoints using support-set performance. Appendix~\ref{app:baseline-details} lists the training recipes and hyperparameters.

\paragraph{Implementation details.}
TimeMorph uses \texttt{meta-llama/Llama-3.2-1B}, \texttt{facebook/dinov2-base}, temporal patch length 16, stride 8, hidden size 128, and the Stage II checkpoint. Downstream adaptation uses 5 warm-up epochs, 55 joint-training epochs, and LoRA with rank 16 and alpha 32. Additional details are in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\subsection{Main Results}

\paragraph{Benchmark-level comparison.}
Table~\ref{tab:ucr_fewshot_paper_current-fewshot-main} shows that TimeMorph obtains the highest mean accuracy under all four support sizes: 48.31, 55.55, 64.14, and 69.16 for 1-, 2-, 5-, and 10-shot. The strongest non-TimeMorph baseline is \textsc{COSCO-ResNet}, with 47.88, 54.95, 63.55, and 67.18. Thus the improvement over the strongest specialized CNN/prototype baseline is modest in accuracy, especially in the lowest-shot regimes, but it is consistent across support sizes and is also reflected in the best \texttt{AvgRank} (4.24 vs. 4.70 for \textsc{COSCO-ResNet}). The gap is more substantial against Transformer-style and LLM-style baselines: TimeMorph improves \texttt{Avg} by 4.53 points over \textsc{Informer} and 18.36 points over \textsc{GPT4TS}. These results suggest that the proposed interface is most clearly beneficial relative to generic sequence and LLM-style baselines, while remaining competitive with strong specialized CNN/prototype methods.

\paragraph{UCR ablations.}
Removing curriculum pretraining lowers \texttt{Avg} by 3.35 points, indicating
that Stage I+II initialization helps when downstream labels are too scarce to
learn both the representation and LLM interface from scratch. Single-view
variants also underperform the full model: removing the morphology branch drops
\texttt{Avg} by 5.08 points, while removing the temporal branch drops it by 4.20
points, supporting the complementarity of chronological and morphological
evidence. Replacing class-token scoring with unconstrained generative adaptation
lowers \texttt{Avg} by 4.15 points, suggesting that the closed-set interface is
more stable for few-shot classification.

\paragraph{Semantic priors and LLM backbone.}
For external datasets with meaningful label names, initializing and regularizing
class-token rows from label verbalizers improves the averaged result by 2.39
points on the compact MIT-BIH/CinC2017 summary, although the gain is not uniform
at every shot; the full semantic-prior table is in
Appendix~\ref{app:semantic-prior-ablation}.

The no-LLM variant in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:ablation-llm-backbone} replaces
the language pathway with a lightweight linear classifier on the same dual-view
features and reduces \texttt{Avg} by 7.83 points. This suggests that the
language pathway contributes beyond the dual-view encoder alone, while not
isolating whether the gain comes from pretrained knowledge, additional capacity,
or token scoring.

\section{Limitations}

TimeMorph improves average accuracy and average rank, but its gains over the strongest CNN/prototype baselines are modest in extremely low-shot settings.
The main empirical message is therefore not that LLM-based TSC now universally dominates specialized CNN-style few-shot classifiers, but that a carefully designed time-series-to-language interface can substantially outperform previous Transformer-style and LLM-style baselines while remaining competitive with strong task-specific methods.
TimeMorph also has a larger inference footprint than compact CNN baselines because it reuses a pretrained causal LLM, even though only a small fraction of parameters is updated during downstream adaptation.
In our efficiency probe, this makes TimeMorph two to three orders of magnitude slower at inference than compact CNN/prototype baselines.
The current UCR pretraining setting also uses unlabeled official TRAIN sequences from a fixed subset of UCR datasets, so it does not establish strict leave-dataset-out transfer for every UCR target dataset.
The external semantic-prior results improve the averaged performance on the two reported semantic-label datasets, but individual shot-level effects still vary, so label semantics should be viewed as a prior over the class-token rows rather than as a replacement for supervised adaptation.
TimeMorph may benefit applications where labeled time-series data are scarce, such as physiological monitoring or industrial inspection, but the present study is not a deployment-ready clinical or safety-critical system.
Any deployment in such domains would require domain-specific validation, privacy review, and failure analysis beyond the scope of this paper.

\paragraph{Morphology-map transform.}
The operator $\Gamma_{\mathrm{img}}$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:morphology-map} applies per-sample min--max normalization over the two-dimensional grid and a fixed contrast adjustment $u\mapsto u^{0.8}$. In the default morphology mode, the effective window length is $\max(1,\lfloor\sqrt{T}\rfloor)$, the stride length is $\max(1,\lfloor 0.1q\rfloor)$, incomplete windows are completed by replicate padding on the left, and the resulting grid is resized to the DINOv2 image resolution with nearest-neighbor interpolation before RGB replication. We use DINOv2 patch tokens from the configured intermediate layer and exclude the class token.

\paragraph{Stage I pretraining hyperparameters.}
Stage I uses mask ratio $0.4$, branch-dropout probability $0.1$, jitter standard deviation $0.03$, scaling range $[0.8,1.2]$, and time-mask ratio $0.1$ for the two augmented views. The Stage I loss weights are $\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}=1.0$, $\lambda_t=0.25$, $\lambda_v=0.25$, and $\lambda_f=0.5$. These values are fixed before the downstream few-shot runs.
